Smart Energy Monitor

A four person team project to measure the voltage, current and power of an AC load and display it live.

Altium Designer ATmega328PB Op Amps Analog Front End 2-Layer PCB
The Task

ELECTENG 209 set the brief: design a working energy monitor that measures the voltage, current and power drawn by a real AC load and displays the result on a 4-digit 7-segment display. Every stage of the course had to work together on one board, sensing, amplification, filtering, power supply and firmware, rather than as separate exercises.

We worked as Team 09, a group of four. I took the hardware: the analog front end and the full PCB layout in Altium, with input into the firmware wherever a hardware decision shaped how a signal needed to be sampled.

Key Objectives

The board needed to:

  • Scale and level shift the AC voltage and current into the ADC's 0 to 5V range
  • Amplify and filter both signals without distorting the 500Hz fundamental
  • Detect the voltage zero crossing in hardware for timing
  • Generate its own regulated 5V rail from the AC input
  • Survive a 2-layer, through-hole, fully hand-soldered build
Prototyping & Iteration

Getting there took a few rounds of recalculating against the real spec rather than trusting a first pass:

  • Derived the diff-amp gains and the offset buffer from the actual 2.5 to 7.5VA load range, not a textbook example
  • Sized the Schmitt trigger's hysteresis band as a trade-off, too little and it chattered, too much and the detected edge drifted from the true crossing
  • Split the ground and 5V copper pours so the power rail couldn't couple noise into the sense signals
  • Traced a bring-up fault, where populating the comparator stage disturbed the offset and 5V rails, back to a solder joint

Design Nuances

PCB design

  • GND pour runs continuously under every signal trace, since return current takes the lowest impedance path back to source, if there's no copper directly beneath, it finds a longer, noisier loop instead
  • 5V pour deliberately isolated from the signal traces, since it bounces a little every time an op amp output swings or the comparator switches, routing anything sensitive too close would couple that bounce straight into the measurement
  • ISENSP, the shunt's hot node, flooded rather than routed as a thin trace, since it carries real load current and any trace resistance there adds directly to the voltage the current amplifier is trying to measure
  • Decoupling caps placed within a few millimetres of the LM324's supply pins, since all four op amp sections share one pin pair and a transient from any one of them shows up on the others otherwise
  • Test points planned before population, which is what let us localize the rail disturbance fault with a scope instead of guessing

Hardware design

  • Kept the small feedback stability cap separate in purpose from the actual anti-aliasing filter, both look like "a filter" on paper, but mixing up their roles would quietly distort the 500Hz signal instead of just cleaning it up
  • Buffered the 2.1V offset with its own op amp stage so one channel's loading can't drag the reference the other channel is measured against
  • Sized the Schmitt trigger's hysteresis as a trade-off between chatter and phase error, and found the resulting error depends only on the hysteresis to signal amplitude ratio, not on line frequency
  • Clamp diodes on the amplifier inputs guard against the specific transient an inductive load throws back through the sense resistors, not generic protection
  • Used firmware interpolation instead of a second hardware comparator for the current zero crossing, since no spare op amp section was available, arguably more accurate anyway since it skips a comparator's own propagation delay and hysteresis error
The Build So Far

The board sits between the AC supply and the load. It senses voltage through a divider and current through a shunt, conditions both with op amps, and the ATmega328PB samples them to calculate RMS voltage, RMS current and power.

Voltage sensing, current sensing, the offset reference and the regulator all check out on the bench. What's left is the firmware: the current channel has no hardware comparator, so its zero crossing has to come from interpolating ADC samples instead, and that piece, along with the display driver, is still being written.

Personal Contributions

My main contributions:

  • Derived the differential amplifier, filter and hysteresis values from the project's real electrical spec
  • Designed and laid out the full 2-layer board in Altium, including the ground/power pour split and decoupling placement
  • Planned labelled test points for every stage ahead of assembly
  • Helped shape the firmware's ADC sampling and current zero-crossing approach once the hardware limits were clear

I also hand-soldered the populated board myself, a dense mix of through-hole op amp, shunt and screw terminal footprints, and did the bring-up debugging with a multimeter and oscilloscope when the comparator stage started disturbing nearby rails.

Reflection

The hardware mostly worked as calculated, which was a relief, but it also meant the harder problems showed up elsewhere. Missing a spare op amp section for a second comparator pushed the current zero crossing into firmware, a workable trade-off, but one I'd avoid next time by budgeting for it before the layout was locked in.

The bring-up issue was the bigger lesson. A correct schematic didn't guarantee a correct board, and it took an actual multimeter on an actual solder joint to find what a review on screen couldn't.

There's still firmware to finish and a board to fully validate, but the parts that are done taught me more about real analog design than any lab did on its own.
